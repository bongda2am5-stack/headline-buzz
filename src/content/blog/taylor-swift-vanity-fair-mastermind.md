Taylor Swift Accused of Being "Regina George" in Vanity Fair's "The Mastermind" Investigation

Vanity Fair has published one of the most talked-about celebrity profiles of the year — and Taylor Swift is at the center of it, even though the magazine never once prints her name. "The Mastermind", written by journalist May Jeong and published on November 23 after a year of reporting and more than 100 interviews, refers to Swift only as "The Star". According to the piece, what emerges from those conversations is a portrait that clashes sharply with the story Swift has told about herself for nearly two decades: not just a musician, not just a celebrity, but — in the profile's words — "a combination of victim and victimizer."

## The school years, revisited

Much of the profile revisits Swift's school years in Pennsylvania, where she has long said she was an outcast who was bullied by classmates. In a 2009 interview with journalist Katie Couric, Swift said that when she was 12, classmates would get up from the lunch table and move somewhere else whenever she sat down beside them. According to Vanity Fair, several of her former classmates remember it differently.

Chanice Skinner, who sat next to Swift in music class, is quoted as saying Swift was "always part of the popular crowd. If you weren't part of her friend group, you didn't exist to her. If anything, you were one of the bullies."

Sharon Luyben, Swift's seventh- and eighth-grade music teacher, also disputed the lunch-table account, telling Jeong that students had assigned seats — which, she argued, made the story impossible. Luyben has since said her words were taken out of context and that she holds only positive memories of her former student.

The detail that has travelled furthest online, however, is a small one. Former classmate Arielle Peppers recalled that Swift owned three pairs of expensive UGG boots in different colours — noting that a single pair cost roughly half of her single mother's weekly wages at the time. Peppers has since spoken out after facing backlash from Swift's fans over the remark, saying she shared "a straightforward childhood memory" with the reporter.

## "It's truly Regina George"

The profile's most explosive passages come from anonymous music-industry sources. One executive, identified only as "The Exec", is quoted as saying: "She is punitive. It's truly Regina George" — a reference to the fictional mean girl from the 2004 film Mean Girls. Another source described as once close to Swift is quoted as saying: "Nobody is beyond reproach with her. If you don't see it her way, she comes after you."

Jeong structures the article like a record with two sides, according to coverage of the piece. Side A presents the familiar version of Swift's life story. Side B introduces the people who, in the magazine's framing, remember things a little differently. The profile also looks back at Swift's rise — from a girl raised on a Christmas tree farm south of Reading, Pennsylvania, to the most powerful woman in the music industry — while questioning how much of the origin story holds up. According to the piece, her father, Scott Swift, was a third-generation banker and a vice president at Merrill Lynch, where he managed an investment portfolio worth approximately $270 million, and invested around half a million dollars in the record label that signed his daughter.

## The money and the silence

The profile also charts the scale of Swift's fortune. According to Vanity Fair, Swift became a billionaire in 2023 thanks to her touring, with a net worth placed at $1.6 billion in the fall of 2025 — and, the magazine added, by the time the issue goes to press she will be worth more than $2 billion.

![A concert crowd under bright stage lights](/images/taylor-swift-vanity-fair-mastermind-05.jpg)

*Photo: Thibault Trillet / Pexels*

Swift declined to comment for the piece, although Vanity Fair said it sent her several questions while reporting it. And the reporting itself has come under scrutiny: Variety chief music critic Chris Willman wrote that despite the claim of more than 100 sources, only six were quoted on the record — a point Swift's defenders have seized on, arguing that a year of reporting produced little that is new or verifiable.

## The backlash — and what happens next

The reaction has been as loud as the profile itself. Swift's fans have rallied around her, while critics of the magazine have accused it of running a coordinated hit piece — and the people quoted in it have found themselves caught in the middle. Peppers, the former classmate behind the UGG boots anecdote, has had to publicly clarify her comments after the backlash, while Luyben has said her remarks were taken out of context.

![Stacks of glossy fashion magazines](/images/taylor-swift-vanity-fair-mastermind-03.jpg)

*Photo: Pixabay / Pexels*

Whether the profile changes anything about how Swift is seen is an open question. Her public image — built over 12 eras, in the magazine's own framing — has survived feuds, lawsuits and a very public battle over her master recordings. What "The Mastermind" does, at minimum, is put a different version of the story on the record, attributed to the people who say they were there.

## A note on this story

Everything in this article is based on Vanity Fair's reporting and on coverage of that reporting. The most serious claims — including the "Regina George" comparison and the accounts of Swift's school years — come from anonymous sources or from former classmates whose memories differ from Swift's own account, and they remain unverified. Swift declined to comment for the profile. Until more is confirmed on the record, the details in "The Mastermind" should be treated as alleged, not established — and, as even some critics of the piece have noted, it is entirely possible that the most dramatic readings of it are overblown.
