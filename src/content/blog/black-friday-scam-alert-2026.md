Black Friday 2026: That $49.99 iPad Is a Trap

Black Friday is here, and the numbers explain why scammers love it. Adobe forecasts that Americans will spend $12.9 billion online today alone — a record for Black Friday, up 9.2% on last year — followed by $15.1 billion on Cyber Monday and $275.1 billion across the holiday season. Where that much money moves in a single weekend, criminals follow. This year, they started weeks early.

Security researchers have been tracking the build-up since October, and the picture is stark. Check Point Research found more than 4,000 new shopping-themed domains registered between 1 October and 8 November 2026, and nearly one in five was already classified as risky. Some were sitting dormant, waiting to be switched on for Black Friday. Bitdefender's Antispam Lab found that half of the Black Friday-themed email campaigns it observed between 30 October and 10 November were scams. The United States accounted for 46% of those scam emails, followed by Germany at 14%, Japan at 9% and Ireland at 9%.

The pattern is not new, but the scale and the polish are. Here is how the scams work this year, and how to check before you pay.

## The fake storefront

The classic Black Friday scam is a website that looks exactly like a real retailer and is not. Check Point's most detailed example this season is a site impersonating Walmart's Rollback savings pages. Its homepage advertised clearance discounts of up to 80%, including an iPad for $49.99, with a rating badge and a claim that only 48 units remained.

At checkout, the site asked for a name, email address, shipping address and phone number. The researchers note the goal may not even be the $49.99: that personal information can be reused for follow-up phishing, identity theft, or resale, so the real damage can arrive weeks after the "purchase".

The same playbook shows up across other brands. Check Point flagged fake storefronts posing as Best Buy, Walmart and Oakley, and fraudulent shopping and delivery sites impersonating Amazon, AliExpress, Temu, DHL, FedEx, USPS and Royal Mail. Bitdefender found paid social-media ads leading to fake storefronts advertising "Black Friday up to 90% off" while posing as fashion and sportswear brands, including Nike, Ugg, Ray-Ban and Michael Kors.

## The email flood

Your inbox is the second front. Half of the Black Friday-themed email campaigns Bitdefender's lab observed in late October and early November were scams — a pattern that mirrors what it found in 2025. The most common lures were luxury goods, electronics, gift cards, giveaways, vouchers and "exclusive" early deals: a luxury handbag sale, a Samsung TV at an impossible price, a "$5,000 Black Friday reward" with a "secret link".

These emails arrive mixed in with genuine marketing from stores you actually shop at, and they can carry the right logo, colours and sender name. What gives them away is where they want you to go: a link to a lookalike domain, a "claim your reward" page asking for payment details, or an unexpected attachment. Never click through from an email to buy — open the retailer's official app or type its address into your browser yourself.

## The delivery text and the payment trap

Fake delivery notifications are a staple of the season, and they work because they arrive exactly when you are expecting real parcels. A text claims your package is held, your address is wrong, or a small fee is needed to release it — and asks you to tap a link or pay up. Do not tap it. Check the order in the retailer's own app or on the courier's official site, using the tracking number from your order confirmation.

Watch, too, for unexpected PayPal invoices or money requests, and for coupon offers that ask for payment information to unlock a supposed free gift. Real codes come from the retailer's own website, app or verified messages — never from a stranger asking for your card details.

## Ten signs to check before you pay

1. The deal is too good to be true. A new iPad for $49.99 with "only 48 left" is a scam. Compare the price with two or three stores you know.
2. You are told to act right now. Countdown timers and "only a few left" warnings are pressure tactics. A real sale will still be there if you come back through the store's own site.
3. The web address is a lookalike. Watch for a brand name joined to a sale word, such as brand-blackfriday.shop, a swapped letter such as amaz0n, or an extra letter such as walmartt.
4. The brand name is in the wrong part of the address. In walmart.deals-today.shop, the site belongs to deals-today.shop, not Walmart.
5. The site is brand new. A store claiming to be a famous retailer but created a few weeks ago is a red flag — free tools such as ICANN Lookup show a domain's creation date.
6. A delivery text asks you to tap a link or pay a fee. Check the order in the retailer's app instead, and forward the text to 7726 (SPAM).
7. A PayPal invoice or money request you did not expect arrives out of nowhere. Log into PayPal directly — never through a link in the message.
8. A coupon or free gift asks for payment information to unlock it. A genuine discount never needs your card details first.
9. The page demands personal details up front. A checkout that wants your phone number, email and address on a site you have never heard of is harvesting data, not selling goods.
10. The deal reaches you through a random social-media post or ad, not the retailer's own page or app. A bargain in a group is not verified just because it has comments and likes.

## What to do if you already paid

Act fast. Contact your bank or card issuer and dispute the charge — the sooner you report it, the better your chances of getting the money back. Change any password you reused on the fake site, and turn on two-factor authentication on your email and shopping accounts. Report the fraud to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (ic3.gov) and the Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov). Keep screenshots of the site, the confirmation message and the charge.

Black Friday's real deals are worth shopping. Adobe's forecast says today will be the biggest online Black Friday ever, and most of that spending will go to genuine stores with genuine discounts. The fake ones are designed to look exactly like them. Check the address, check the price against a store you trust, and never let a countdown timer make the decision for you.
