Five thousand one hundred and fifty-eight days. That is how long Faith Hedgepeth's family waited.

On the morning of September 7, 2012, the 19-year-old University of North Carolina sophomore was found dead in the off-campus apartment she shared with her friend Karena Rosario in Chapel Hill. She had been beaten to death. She was a member of the Haliwa-Saponi Tribe, a young woman with a whole life in front of her, and within hours her killing had become the kind of case that haunts a town.

Then it went cold. For fourteen years, it stayed that way.

On October 23, 2026, in a courtroom in Durham, North Carolina, a jury finally gave the case an ending. After a 19-day trial and six hours of deliberation spread over two days, the jury found Miguel Enrique Salguero-Olivares guilty on all four counts: first-degree murder, first-degree burglary, first-degree rape and first-degree sexual offense. Judge Keith Gregory sentenced him that same afternoon to life in prison without the possibility of parole, plus 45 to 65 additional years for the other charges, and lifetime registration as a sex offender.

It took fourteen years. It took a traffic stop, a palm print and a science that did not exist when she died.

## A Case That Would Not Break

From the start, investigators had evidence. They had DNA from the scene. They had a palm print on a wine bottle. They had a handwritten note on a fast-food bag — a taunting message left behind at the scene — and DNA on that too.

What they did not have was a name. The DNA matched no one in any database. Investigators collected more than 400 DNA samples from people connected to the case, and not one of them matched. Years passed. The trail went cold.

In 2016, investigators tried something new. Using the DNA from the scene, they built a composite image of the man it belonged to — a face drawn from genetics — and released it to the public, along with a $40,000 reward. Still nothing. The case sat unsolved for nine years, one of the most frustrating cold cases in North Carolina.

Faith's family never stopped waiting. Her parents, Connie and Roland Hedgepeth, kept her name alive. Her tribe, the Haliwa-Saponi, kept her memory alive. And her case became part of a wider, painful conversation about violence against Indigenous women — a pattern in which cases like hers too often go unsolved.

## The Traffic Stop

The break came from somewhere nobody expected: a routine traffic stop.

In September 2021, Salguero-Olivares was pulled over for driving while intoxicated. As part of that stop, police took a DNA sample. When it was run against the evidence from the Hedgepeth crime scene, it matched.

Investigators had also used genetic genealogy — tracing the crime-scene DNA through family trees — to narrow the search to him. The traffic stop gave them the sample that confirmed it. He was arrested in September 2021, nine years after the killing.

The evidence the prosecution would take to trial was built from the pieces that had been there all along. The DNA from a sexual assault kit matched. The palm print on the wine bottle matched. The DNA on the handwritten note matched. At trial, forensic scientist Erin Ermish told the jury that DNA swabbed from Hedgepeth's body was 170 septillion times more likely to be a mixture of DNA from Hedgepeth and Salguero-Olivares than from Hedgepeth and an unknown individual. It was, the prosecution said, evidence that was "screaming his name."

## The Trial

Jury selection began on September 28, 2026. Over the next 19 days, 23 witnesses testified over nine days of evidence. Salguero-Olivares, 33, did not testify. His lawyers rested their case on October 21 without calling a single witness.

The defense argued that investigators had suffered from "tunnel vision" — that they had decided early on that their client was a monster, and had stopped looking anywhere else. They suggested he had had consensual sex with the victim days before her death, and they pointed to a blood swab they said police had lost. No witnesses corroborated the consensual-sex claim.

Assistant District Attorney Angela Garcia-Lamarca closed the case for the prosecution with words the courtroom would not forget. The defendant, she told the jury, "was the jury, he was the judge, and he was the executioner." Then: "He sentenced her to death. He sentenced everybody else who loved her to life."

The jury began deliberating on Thursday, October 23, around 3:30 in the afternoon. They asked to see the reports on the latent prints and the DNA. At about 4:26 p.m., they returned. Guilty on all four counts. First-degree murder — both premeditated and under the felony murder rule. Guilty of burglary, rape and sexual offense. The district attorney had decided not to seek the death penalty, so the murder conviction carried a mandatory sentence: life in prison without parole.

At sentencing, Salguero-Olivares said, "It's not fair." His lawyers have since filed a notice of appeal, and on November 3 they asked for a new trial.

## Forgiveness in Open Court

The most unexpected moment of the day did not come from the jury, or the judge, or the defendant. It came from Faith's parents.

Connie and Roland Hedgepeth spoke in open court after the verdict, and they said they forgave the man convicted of killing their daughter — "without reservation." It was, in the words of one report, the strongest verdict of the day: a family that had waited 5,158 days choosing forgiveness at the very moment the law delivered its harshest sentence.

Judge Gregory, sentencing Salguero-Olivares, noted quietly that he has a son only a year younger than Faith who also attended UNC. The case had never been abstract to the people in that courtroom.

## Why This Case Matters

The Hedgepeth verdict is a story about a single murder and a single family. It is also a story about how cold cases are changing.

When Faith Hedgepeth died in 2012, the DNA at the scene was a dead end — a profile with no name attached. Nine years later, genetic genealogy gave that profile a family tree, and a traffic stop gave it a face. The same science that built a composite image from DNA in 2016 is now routinely used to identify people who believed they would never be found.

Not every cold case ends this way. Many never do. But the message of this one is hard to miss: the evidence does not expire. The DNA does not forget. And fourteen years, it turns out, is not forever.

For Faith Hedgepeth's family, the wait is over. The man a jury found responsible for her death will never walk free again.
