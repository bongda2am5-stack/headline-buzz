There were 111 seconds left on Thanksgiving night, the score was tied at 10, and the Kansas City Chiefs were backed up at their own 9-yard line. Third down, ten yards to go. Patrick Mahomes dropped back, fired a pass over the middle, and Rashee Rice went up and came down with it.

The officials on the field ruled it a catch. First down, Kansas City. The Chiefs had a chance to drive for a win.

Then replay got involved. A few minutes later, the call was gone. Incomplete. Punt.

Buffalo won 13-10 on a 57-yard field goal by Tyler Bass as time expired. But nobody is talking about the kick. They are talking about the catch that was not a catch.

![Rashee Rice runs with the ball for the Kansas City Chiefs](/images/rashee-rice-overturned-catch-chiefs-bills-01.jpg)

## The Play

It was third-and-10 from the Kansas City 9, with 1:51 remaining in the fourth quarter. Mahomes found Rice across the middle for what the crew on the field judged to be an 11-yard completion. Rice had leapt high to make the grab, and he came down with his hands still on the ball. The tip of the football touched the turf as he hit the ground, but the ball itself barely moved.

That was enough for the replay officials in New York. After a review, they ruled that Rice had lost control of the football as he went to the ground, and the completion was wiped out.

NFL vice president of instant replay Mark Butterworth explained the decision in the postgame pool report. As Rice was going to the ground, he said, the receiver had the ball in his right hand, and when he contacted the ground, his hand slid up the ball. The league deemed that a loss of control.

Under NFL rules, the ball touching the ground does not automatically make a pass incomplete — not if the receiver keeps control. That distinction is exactly what has everyone arguing. The replay showed the tip of the ball hitting the field. What it did not clearly show was Rice actually losing the ball.

![Rashee Rice in action for the Chiefs](/images/rashee-rice-overturned-catch-chiefs-bills-02.jpg)

## The Coach Who Disagreed

Chiefs head coach Andy Reid did not hide his frustration after the game. He confirmed the reversal had come from the league office in New York, not from the officials working the game.

"This came from New York," Reid said. "Our guys called it a completion, the guys that were working the game. I'm not quite sure what they saw. I saw he had control of the ball, and his elbow hit then the ball hit, but he had control. Normally, in those kind of situations, it has to be clear and obvious. To me, that was not clear and obvious."

That last phrase — "clear and obvious" — is the standard replay is supposed to meet before it overturns a call made on the field. Reid's point was simple: if the evidence is not clear and obvious, the call on the field should stand. In his view, this one did not come close to that bar.

Even the NBC broadcast team was unsure in the moment. Announcers Mike Tirico and Cris Collinsworth both noted that the ball "barely moved." Former NFL officiating expert Terry McAulay, working the broadcast, believed the call on the field should have stood.

## The Players Who Piled In

The reaction from former players was swift, loud and almost unanimous — and it crossed team lines.

Terrell Owens, a Hall of Fame wide receiver who played for both Reid in Kansas City and for the Bills, was the loudest voice. He posted on X that the reversal was indefensible, and he did it while openly admitting he had been rooting for Buffalo.

"If that wasn't a catch, NOTHING is," Owens wrote. "And I'm actually barracking for my Buffalo Bills!!! But these calls by refs are beyond ridiculous. This is why people are talking about games being rigged."

![Terrell Owens speaking at an event](/images/rashee-rice-overturned-catch-chiefs-bills-03.jpg)

Former quarterback and Heisman Trophy winner Robert Griffin III agreed. "Chiefs fans HAVE EVERY REASON TO BE UPSET. THAT WAS A CATCH BY RASHEE RICE," he wrote.

Former Chiefs wide receiver Gehrig Dieter focused on the same detail everyone else kept coming back to: how little the ball actually moved. "The ball literally does not move! That's such a bad call," he wrote. Chad Johnson and former Chiefs quarterback Kurt Benkert also criticised the decision.

Brittany Mahomes, the quarterback's wife, added her own verdict on social media. "HAHAHAHA WHAT A JOKE," she wrote. "Guess we can't even play football without it getting called against us... not sure what an actual catch is after that I guess?"

![Patrick Mahomes, the Chiefs quarterback](/images/rashee-rice-overturned-catch-chiefs-bills-04.jpg)

## What Happened Next

The reversal forced Kansas City to punt from its own 9-yard line. Buffalo took over at the Kansas City 47 with 1:40 left, already on the Chiefs' side of the field. The Bills could not convert, and they punted the ball back. Kansas City went three-and-out, and a Matt Araiza punt was partially blocked by Buffalo special teams ace Joe Andreessen. Returner Greg Dortch brought it back to the Kansas City 39 with four seconds left.

That was all the room Bass needed. He drilled the 57-yarder as time expired, and the Bills walked off with a 13-10 win that moved them to 10-1 and kept them on top of the AFC. The Chiefs fell to 8-3, their third loss of the season.

![Rice hauls in a touchdown — the kind of catch at the heart of the Thanksgiving debate](/images/rashee-rice-overturned-catch-chiefs-bills-05.jpg)

It had been a strange, rain-soaked night at Highmark Stadium. Bass had tied the game at 10-10 with a 54-yard field goal early in the fourth quarter, and Kansas City kicker Harrison Butker had missed a field goal attempt at the end of an 11-play Chiefs drive. Bills quarterback Josh Allen completed only 10 of 21 passes for 81 yards and threw two interceptions. Rice finished the night with just two catches for 19 yards.

None of that is what anyone remembers. The advanced numbers only underline how big the moment was: according to analytics site RBSDM, the overturned play cost Kansas City 0.6 expected points and lifted Buffalo's win probability from 60.5 percent to 69.2 percent.

## The Question That Will Not Go Away

This is not really an argument about one play. It is the same argument the NFL has been having for years: what, exactly, is a catch?

The league has rewritten the rule, clarified it, and rewritten it again. Still, every season produces a play like this one — a receiver who appears to catch the ball, goes to the ground, and has the completion taken away because of a detail that looks different in slow motion than it did at full speed. The rule is supposed to bring clarity. Too often, it brings the opposite.

Owens went further than most, tying the call to the word that now follows every controversial decision in the sport: rigged. "There's more money than ever floating around on NFL games, and the ref issue is getting worse by the week," he wrote in a follow-up post. He was not literally accusing the league of fixing the game. He was saying something that worries the NFL far more: that fans are starting to believe it could.

That is the real cost of a call like this. The Bills earned their win — they made the plays at the end, and Bass's kick was as clutch as it gets. But when a game turns on a replay decision that even a Hall of Famer rooting for the winning team calls "beyond ridiculous," the conversation stops being about the football. It becomes about whether anyone can trust what they are watching.

So, was it a catch? The officials in New York say no. Almost everyone else who watched it says yes. What do you think?
