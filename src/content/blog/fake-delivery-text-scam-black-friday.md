Your phone buzzes. "USPS: Your package could not be delivered because of an incomplete address. Update your details within 12 hours."

It feels real, because it arrives at the exact moment it would be real. It is Black Friday weekend. You have ordered things. You are waiting for packages. A message about a package lands on someone who believes it — and right now, that is almost everyone.

It is fake. The package does not exist. The fee is not the point. The link is.

![A woman anxiously checks her phone](/images/fake-delivery-text-scam-black-friday-01.jpg)

## The Scale of It

This is not one viral post or a single scammer. It is an industry, and it peaks every holiday season. FortiGuard Labs, the research arm of cybersecurity firm Fortinet, found more than 24,000 newly registered holiday-related domains in the run-up to this year's shopping season — and classified more than 4,400 of them as malicious. Shipping and delivery terms appeared in nearly 35 percent of those newly registered domains. The words "Black Friday" appeared in just 1.64 percent.

That tells you where the criminals are aiming. Not at the sale itself, but at the box that is supposed to arrive after it.

The same report documented lookalike domains impersonating the big carriers — USPS, UPS, FedEx and DHL — and a fraudulent storefront that copied Walmart's logo, colours and product listings to advertise Black Friday deals. From August through October 2026, researchers also collected about 1.72 million stolen login records tied to seven major e-commerce platforms. Check Point researchers separately flagged thousands of fake shopping domains registered ahead of the weekend.

The timing is deliberate. A delivery text in July is easy to ignore. A delivery text in late November, when half the country is tracking a parcel, is almost impossible to ignore. That is the whole trick.

![A man worries over a message on his phone](/images/fake-delivery-text-scam-black-friday-02.jpg)

## How the Scam Works

The message varies, but the story is always the same. A delivery has a problem: the address is incomplete, the label is damaged, postage is missing, or the package is "on hold." To fix it, you must open a link within a few hours, confirm your address and pay a small fee — usually a dollar or two.

The link opens a website that looks like the real USPS site. The Federal Trade Commission describes it exactly that way: "a website that looks like the real USPS site — but isn't." The page asks for your address, then your card number to pay the fee.

The fee was never the point. A dollar ninety-nine is nothing. What the criminals want is the card number, the name, the address and the phone number — everything needed to use your card, or to steal your identity and open accounts in your name. The same script runs under other brand names too: UPS, FedEx, DHL, Amazon, and more recently toll roads, with messages about an "unpaid toll" and a fine if you do not pay now.

The U.S. Postal Inspection Service, the law enforcement arm of USPS, warns about exactly this on its smishing page — smishing is phishing that arrives by text message. It is not one campaign. The messages come from constantly changing phone numbers, and the websites behind them disappear within days. That is why the advice is always the same: do not engage with the message at all.

## The One Rule That Exposes It

Here is the rule that kills this scam every time, and it comes straight from the Postal Service itself.

USPS does not text you unless you asked for tracking, with a tracking number — and those texts never contain a link. Tracking is free. USPS does not charge a fee for redelivery. A message that wants you to click a link and pay a "redelivery fee" is not from the Postal Service. Full stop.

The Federal Communications Commission put it even more bluntly in guidance updated on November 18, 2026: USPS tracking texts go out only if you sign up, and those messages will not contain links. "If you get a text with a link, the text is fake."

The other carriers say the same thing. FedEx does not send unsolicited texts asking for money or personal details, and it never requests payment or personal information to release a package. UPS will never request payment, personal information or banking details through a text or email. If a message from any of them asks for money to release a parcel, it is not from them.

There is also a quick check you can do before you tap anything: look at the web address in the message, specifically the part right before the first single slash. A link like "usps.com-redelivery.example" is not usps.com. The real domain is the part immediately before that first slash — and if it is not a domain you recognise, do not open it.

## What It Costs

The numbers explain why this keeps happening. According to the FTC, Americans reported losing $470 million to scams that started with a text message in 2024 — more than five times the figure from 2020. Fake package delivery problems were the most reported type of text scam that year. The FBI reported that Americans lost $503 million to non-payment and non-delivery scams in 2025, and shopping scams were the most reported scam on social media.

It is not a fringe problem. In a November 24 alert, the U.S. Postal Service cited a 2026 AARP survey finding that 84 percent of American consumers experienced or were targeted by at least one form of fraud during the holidays. The U.S. Secret Service, in its November 2026 holiday advisory, warned that scammers build websites that look like popular shopping sites to steal personal and payment information — and that they can even pay to make those fake sites appear at the top of search results. The FBI's Boston Division added in a November 24 alert that flashy websites can be set up and taken down quickly, and that one of the most common holiday frauds is the non-delivery scam: you pay, and the item never arrives.

## What To Do When the Text Arrives

The advice is short, and it works.

Do not tap the link. Open the carrier's own website or app yourself and paste in the tracking number from your order confirmation. If there is no tracking number, there is no package. If the retailer has no record of a shipment, there is nothing to fix.

Then report it. Forward the suspicious text to 7726 — that spells SPAM on a phone keypad — and report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. A fake Postal Service text can also be reported to spam@uspis.gov, and a fake FedEx message to abuse@fedex.com. Then delete the message.

Cyber Monday is November 30, and the delivery texts will spike again as the orders from this weekend start moving. The rule does not change: if a delivery text contains a link, or asks for a fee, it is fake. Check your order in the retailer's own app. That is the only tracking page that matters.
